Add your records

Each person submits their own. The engine does the rest.

Nobody in this family has to organize anything. Sign in with your invite, drop what you have in whatever shape it exists, fill in your own short profile, and stop. Sorting, matching, and placing people on the tree is the engine's job, not yours.

Choose files

    The daylight rule: a straight phone photo in good light beats a perfect scan that never happens. If a photo has names written on the back, photograph the back too. Funeral programs are gold: one page can carry parents, siblings, a church, and a burial place all at once.

    Already public? Skip it. The Dawes Rolls, the census, and the Indian Census Rolls live inside Our LGCY as the reference base. Nobody should ever upload what the archive already holds.

    What happens next

    1. It lands untouched

      Your uploads are stored exactly as you sent them, tagged with your name. Originals are never edited and never deleted.

    2. The engine reads and sorts

      Documents are read for names, dates, places, parents, spouses, churches, and cemeteries. A GEDCOM export becomes people and families in one pass. Your facts are checked against the reference corpus and against what your cousins have already sent.

    3. Matches get proposed, not forced

      When five cousins upload the same uncle under three spellings, the engine proposes one person, with the evidence shown. Sure matches merge. Borderline ones wait for a human, and conflicting facts are kept side by side, never overwritten.

    4. You get the payoff

      A message in plain words: "Your upload placed you here. We matched three records to your grandmother. One question only you can answer." Answer it, and the tree grows again.

    Your five minute profile

    Just you, as you know yourself

    You only speak for your own household. Your full name and maiden name, your birth date and place, your parents' names, your partner, your children, and the towns your people lived in before. Approximate is fine. Maiden names matter most of all: they are how the women's lines connect, and they are the first thing families lose.
